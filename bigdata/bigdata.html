Stylometric Analysis of 19th Century French Novels with Spark NLP

Author

Luxuan HUANG

1. Data Description and Extraction Pipeline

1.1 Corpus Composition

The corpus consists of 29 French novels from two literary movements:

  • Realist – 19 novels by Émile Zola, most of them belonging to the Rougon‑Macquart cycle.
  • Romantic – 10 works by Victor Hugo, including major novels (e.g., Notre‑Dame de Paris, Les Misérables) as well as shorter pieces such as Bug‑Jargal and Claude Gueux.

All texts were retrieved from Project Gutenberg in UTF‑8 plain text format.

1.2 Extraction Logic

The goal of the extraction is to obtain only the narrative body of each novel, stripping away Gutenberg’s header, table of contents, and legal notices. This is achieved by:

  1. Checking if the cleaned file already exists in book_data/ (to avoid re‑downloading).
  2. Downloading the raw UTF‑8 file from Project Gutenberg once per unique URL (several short Hugo texts share the same URL).
  3. Extracting the text between the custom start and end tokens defined above.
  4. Saving the cleaned excerpt as book_data/<book_title>.txt.

The extraction pipeline is fully reproducible; running the code below on any machine with internet access will recreate the identical book_data/ directory.

After execution, the book_data/ folder contains 29 cleaned .txt files, one per novel.

2. Load and Transform Pipeline

The cleaned texts must be converted into a Spark DataFrame suitable for annotation. Given the 4 GB RAM constraint, each novel is split into chunks of 3000 words. This keeps individual rows small enough to be processed by the NLP pipeline without triggering out‑of‑memory errors.

The loading step creates a DataFrame with two columns: book (the short title) and text (a chunk).

This simple whitespace‑based split preserves the raw surface form of the text, which is exactly what the NLP pipeline expects. The chunk size of 3000 was chosen empirically to keep individual rows small enough to avoid out‑of‑memory errors, while still providing enough context for the sentence detector and part‑of‑speech tagger to work accurately.

No separate “load all books” DataFrame is created. Instead, loading, chunking, and annotation are performed book‑by‑book – an approach that fits both our memory constraints and the embarrassingly parallel nature of the task.

3. Annotation Pipeline

Annotation is performed with Spark NLP’s pretrained pipeline “explain_document_ml”. This model provides: Sentence detection Tokenization Lemmatization Part‑of‑speech tagging

It is multilingual (hence the ml suffix) and works out of the box for French.

We launch Spark through sparknlp.start() with a heap limit of 1 GB and set spark.sql.shuffle.partitions to 1 to minimize shuffle overhead on a single‑machine setup. For each novel we: Read the cleaned text from book_data/. Split it into 3000‑word chunks. Create a small DataFrame with one column text. Apply the pretrained pipeline. Write the resulting annotated DataFrame to Parquet (overwrite mode). Discard the data and move to the next book.

4. Stylometric Analysis

This section presents a stylometric comparison between the Realist (Émile Zola) and Romantic (Victor Hugo) sub-corpora. All analyzed texts are English translations of the original French works. To ensure data integrity, metrics were computed using decoupled PySpark aggregations before applying readability formulas.

4.1 Readability Indices Comparison

To evaluate syntactic and lexical complexity, the Flesch-Kincaid (FK) readability formula was applied to the corpus.

Figure 4.1: Flesch-Kincaid Readability Indices per Novel grouped by Literary School.

As illustrated in Figure 4.1, the FK scores for the translated novels generally fall within the 65 to 80 range. A notable observation is the difference in variance between the two literary movements. The Realist corpus exhibits greater internal variance; for instance, texts such as L’Argent and La Curée record lower readability scores (below 70), indicating denser syntactic structures or highly specialized vocabulary. Conversely, the Romantic corpus demonstrates a more tightly clustered distribution, suggesting a relatively consistent baseline of textual complexity across the provided translations.

4.2 Stability of Readability Indices over Sliding Windows

To assess the dynamic stability of the readability index throughout the narrative progression, a sliding window approach was implemented.

Figure 4.2: Sliding Window Flesch-Kincaid Readability with a window size of 2000 tokens.

Figure 4.2 displays the FK scores calculated over a window size of 2000 tokens. The light grey lines represent the entire corpus, establishing a baseline volatility for the 19th-century translations. Highlighting specific representative texts reveals macro-structural features. For example, the extended x-axis for Les Misérables accurately reflects its massive token count compared to other works. Additionally, sharp local drops in the FK score (such as the distinct dip in Notre-Dame around the 100k token mark) suggest dense narrative transitions, likely corresponding to complex descriptive passages where sentence lengths naturally increase.

4.3 Zipf’s Law Distribution

To verify the statistical distribution of the vocabulary, the rank-frequency distribution of the lemmatized tokens was analyzed on a log-log scale for each document.

Figure 4.3: Zipf Distribution per Document plotted on a log-log scale.

Figure 4.3 illustrates a highly consistent power-law distribution across the entire corpus. The black dashed line represents the ideal Zipfian slope of -1.Correlation with Text Length: A clear horizontal stratification is observed. The curves extending furthest to the right correspond to the longest epic novels in the corpus, specifically Hugo’s Les Misérables and Zola’s La Débâcle. This extension indicates a larger absolute vocabulary size (higher total number of unique ranks), as expected for works of such magnitude.Short-Form Works: Conversely, the curves situated at the leftmost part of the distribution represent shorter works, such as Hugo’s Claude Gueux, Le Dernier Jour d’un Condamné, Han d’Islande, and Bug-Jargal. These plots truncate at lower ranks (around \(10^3\)), reflecting the limited lexical diversity inherent in shorter narrative forms.Slope Consistency: Despite the significant differences in document length and literary movement, all 29 curves remain parallel to each other and closely align with the ideal slope of -1. This structural parallelism demonstrates that the statistical “economy of language”—the ratio between high-frequency functional words and low-frequency content words—remains invariant across the Realist and Romantic sub-corpora.

4.4 Sentence Length: Dialogue vs. Narration

Sentences were categorized into dialogue and narration to compare structural differences. The classification relied on a heuristic rule checking for the presence of quotation marks (” or ’’).

Figure 4.4: Average Sentence Length: Dialogue vs. Narration by Literary School.

Figure 4.4 presents an unexpected data pattern: sentences classified as dialogue are, on average, longer than narrative sentences in both literary schools. Rather than reflecting an actual 19th-century stylistic feature, this anomaly highlights the limitations of the employed heuristic rule. Long narrative sentences that contain brief embedded quotes are entirely classified as dialogue, which artificially inflates the average length of the dialogue category. Consequently, while the data pipeline executed accurately, it demonstrates that simple string-matching heuristics are insufficient for precise dialogue extraction in complex literary texts.

# Helper function: split a text into word-based chunks
# =====================================================================
def split_text(text, chunk_size=3000):
    """
    Split a long string into a list of chunks,
    each containing approximately `chunk_size` words.
    """
    words = text.split()
    chunks = []
    for i in range(0, len(words), chunk_size):
        chunks.append(" ".join(words[i:i+chunk_size]))
    return chunks

explain_document_pipeline = PretrainedPipeline("explain_document_ml")

# Process each book independently
for book_title in all_urls:
    file_path = os.path.join("book_data", book_title + ".txt")
    if not os.path.exists(file_path):
        print(f"Missing {file_path}, skipping")
        continue

    with open(file_path, "r", encoding="utf-8") as f:
        book_text = f.read()

    # Chunk the text
    chunks = split_text(book_text, chunk_size=3000)
    book_data = [[chunk] for chunk in chunks]

    # Create a DataFrame for this book only
    df = spark.createDataFrame(book_data).toDF("text")

    # Run the NLP pipeline
    result = explain_document_pipeline.transform(df)

    # Persist annotated data to Parquet
    result.write.mode("overwrite").parquet(f"results/{book_title}")
    print(f"Done: {book_title}")

print("All 29 novels processed!")

# ---------- Helper: Load Corpus ----------
def load_annotated_corpus(results_dir="results"):
    first = True
    for folder in os.listdir(results_dir):
        path = os.path.join(results_dir, folder)
        if not os.path.isdir(path):
            continue
        df = spark.read.parquet(path)
        df = df.withColumn("book", F.lit(folder))   
        if first:
            all_df = df
            first = False
        else:
            all_df = all_df.unionByName(df)
    return all_df

annotated_df = load_annotated_corpus()

# ---------- Helper: English Syllable Counting ----------
def english_syllable_count(word):
    word = word.lower()
    if len(word) <= 3: return 1
    word = re.sub(r'(?:[^laeiouy]es|ed|[^laeiouy]e)$', '', word)
    word = re.sub(r'^y', '', word)
    matches = re.findall(r'[aeiouy]{1,2}', word)
    return max(1, len(matches))

english_syllable_udf = F.udf(english_syllable_count, IntegerType())

chunk_stats = annotated_df.select(
    "book",
    F.size("sentence").alias("num_sentences"),
    "token.result"
).withColumnRenamed("result", "tokens")

    "school",
    F.when(F.col("book").isin(zola_books), "Realist").otherwise("Romantic")
)

# 句子统计
sentences_per_book = chunk_stats.groupBy("book", "school").agg(
    F.sum("num_sentences").alias("total_sentences")
)

# 单词和音节统计
tokens_exploded = chunk_stats.select("book", F.explode("tokens").alias("token_word"))
tokens_exploded = tokens_exploded.withColumn("syllables", english_syllable_udf(F.col("token_word")))

words_per_book = tokens_exploded.groupBy("book").agg(
    F.count("token_word").alias("total_words"),
    F.sum("syllables").alias("total_syllables")
)

# 合并并计算 Flesch-Kincaid
book_agg = sentences_per_book.join(words_per_book, on="book")

book_indices = book_agg.withColumn(
    "FK_Score",
    F.round(206.835 - 1.015 * (F.col("total_words") / F.col("total_sentences")) - 
            84.6 * (F.col("total_syllables") / F.col("total_words")), 2)
)

pandas_indices = book_indices.toPandas()

# 绘图：按流派区分颜色

# A.4.2 Sliding Window Readability (All Data + Highlight)

def sliding_window_readability(book_name, window_size=500, step=250):
    tokens_df = annotated_df.filter(F.col("book") == book_name).select("token")
    all_words = []
    for row in tokens_df.collect():
        for token_ann in row.token:
            all_words.append(token_ann.result)
    
    results = []
    for start in range(0, len(all_words) - window_size + 1, step):
        window_words = all_words[start:start+window_size]
        sentences = sum(1 for w in window_words if w.endswith(('.', '!', '?')))
        if sentences == 0:
            continue
        
        word_count = len(window_words)
        syllable_count = sum(english_syllable_count(w) for w in window_words)
        score = 206.835 - 1.015 * (word_count / sentences) - 84.6 * (syllable_count / word_count)
            
        results.append({"position": start, "Score": score})
    return pd.DataFrame(results)

from pyspark.sql import Window, functions as F
import plotly.express as px
import plotly.graph_objects as go

print("開始處理 A.4.3 Zipf's Law 分析...")

# 檢查字段是否存在並提取單詞
cols = annotated_df.columns
if "lemmas" in cols:
    print("使用 lemmas.result 進行統計...")
    words_df = annotated_df.select("book", F.explode("lemmas.result").alias("word"))
elif "token" in cols:
    print("未找到 lemmas，使用 token.result 並轉小寫進行統計...")
    words_df = annotated_df.select("book", F.explode("token.result").alias("word"))
    words_df = words_df.withColumn("word", F.lower(F.col("word")))
else:
    print("錯誤：找不到可用的文本字段！請檢查標註流程。")
    words_df = None

if words_df:
    # 過濾掉標點和過短的詞
    words_df = words_df.filter(F.length(F.col("word")) > 1)

    # 統計詞頻
    word_counts = words_df.groupBy("book", "word").agg(F.count("*").alias("frequency"))

    # 計算排名
    window_spec = Window.partitionBy("book").orderBy(F.desc("frequency"))
    word_ranked = word_counts.withColumn("rank", F.row_number().over(window_spec))

    # 轉為 Pandas 並繪圖
    zipf_pd = word_ranked.toPandas()
    
    if len(zipf_pd) > 0:
        fig_zipf = px.line(zipf_pd, x="rank", y="frequency", color="book", 
                           log_x=True, log_y=True, 
                           title="Zipf Distribution per Document (Log-Log Scale)")
        
        # 添加理論斜率參考線
        max_f = zipf_pd['frequency'].max()
        fig_zipf.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=[1, 10000], y=[max_f, max_f/10000],
                                      mode="lines", line=dict(dash="dash", color="black"), 
                                      name="Ideal slope -1"))
        
        fig_zipf.write_html("plots/zipf.html")
        print("✅ plots/zipf.html")
    else:
        print("⚠ ：Zipf 數據為空，未生成圖表。")

# ============================================================
# A.4.4 Dialogue vs. Narration Segmentation
# ============================================================
def is_dialogue(sentence_text):
    if sentence_text is None: return False
    # 由于全是英文，主要依赖双引号和单引号进行启发式判断
    return ('"' in sentence_text) or ("''" in sentence_text)

is_dialogue_udf = F.udf(is_dialogue, BooleanType())

sentences_df = annotated_df.select(
    "book",
    F.explode("sentence.result").alias("sentence")
)
sentences_df = sentences_df.withColumn("dialogue", is_dialogue_udf(F.col("sentence")))
sentences_df = sentences_df.withColumn("length", F.length(F.col("sentence")))

sentences_df = sentences_df.withColumn(
    "school",
    F.when(F.col("book").isin(zola_books), "Realist").otherwise("Romantic")
)

summary = sentences_df.groupBy("school", "dialogue").agg(
    F.avg("length").alias("avg_length"),
    F.count("*").alias("sentence_count")
).toPandas()

summary["category"] = summary["dialogue"].map({True: "Dialogue", False: "Narration"})

fig_dialogue = px.bar(summary, x="school", y="avg_length", color="category", barmode="group",
                      title="Average Sentence Length: Dialogue vs. Narration by Literary School",
                      labels={"avg_length": "Avg Characters per Sentence", "school": "Literary Movement"})
fig_dialogue.write_html("plots/dialogue.html")

print("All stylometric analyses complete!")
